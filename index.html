Fluency & speech practice

Techniques for easier, more relaxed speech.

Speech-language therapy may use different strategies to help a person manage speaking tension, pace and fluency. The most suitable techniques depend on each person’s goals and are selected with the therapist.

Practice with guidance. These techniques are examples of approaches that may be explored during therapy. They are not a checklist everyone needs to use, and they should feel comfortable rather than forced.

01

Breathing, relaxation & release

Build awareness of breathing and reduce unnecessary tension before and during speech.

  • Diaphragmatic breathingGentle, comfortable breathing that allows the abdomen to move naturally.
  • Slow, controlled exhalationLetting air out steadily without pushing or holding the breath.
  • Shoulder relaxationReleasing raised or tightened shoulders before speaking.
  • Neck muscle relaxationNoticing and easing excess neck tension, without forceful stretching.
  • Jaw relaxationKeeping the jaw comfortable and avoiding clenching.
  • Throat relaxationAiming for an easy, unstrained feeling in the throat and voice.
  • Tension-release techniqueRecognising where effort builds up and practising a gentler release.
02

Gentle voice & speech starts

Explore softer, smoother ways to begin and connect sounds.

  • Gentle hummingEasy, comfortable humming to notice voice vibration without strain.
  • Easy onsetBeginning voicing gently rather than starting with a hard push.
  • Light articulatory contactUsing light, rather than forceful, contact of the lips and tongue for sounds.
  • Continuous phonationPractising smooth voice flow between connected sounds where appropriate.
  • Gentle prolonged speechStretching speech sounds slightly and comfortably to practise smoother flow.
03

Pacing & phrasing

Make room for natural pauses and manageable speech groups.

  • Pausing and phrasingBreaking longer thoughts into meaningful phrases with natural pauses.
  • Reduced speech rateFinding a comfortable pace that supports clarity without sounding unnatural.
  • Relaxed conversational speechTransferring comfortable strategies into everyday conversations.
04

Stuttering modification & confidence

Build awareness, reduce struggle and practise responding to moments of stuttering.

  • Pull-out techniqueWhen appropriate, easing out of a stutter with less tension while continuing the word.
  • Cancellation techniqueAfter a stutter, pausing briefly and trying the word again with reduced effort.
  • Voluntary stutteringPractising chosen, gentle stuttering in a safe setting to reduce fear or avoidance, if suitable.
  • Desensitisation practiceGradually working on comfort and confidence around speaking situations.
  • Positive self-talkUsing realistic, compassionate thoughts to support confidence and participation.

Some strategies focus on speech fluency, while others support relaxation, confidence or communication participation. A speech-language therapist can help decide what fits your needs.

Frequently asked questions

Questions about speech therapy?

It’s normal to have questions before starting therapy. Here are a few common ones. If your question isn’t covered, please get in touch.

Ask a question
What is speech and language therapy?

It is support for communication and, when within the therapist’s training and scope, feeding or swallowing needs. Depending on the person, sessions may work on speech sounds, understanding and using language, fluency, voice or social communication.

How do I know if my child may need support?

If you have concerns about your child’s speech, understanding, use of words, interaction or communication, you can discuss them with a speech-language therapist. An assessment can help clarify strengths, needs and possible next steps.

Can teenagers and adults attend therapy?

Yes. Speech-language therapy can support people across the lifespan. For teens and adults, goals may relate to stammering, voice, speech clarity, language or communication after an illness or injury.

What happens in the first session?

The first appointment typically involves discussing your concerns, communication history and goals. Depending on the situation, the therapist may recommend an assessment and explain possible support options.

How long does therapy take?

There is no single timeline. It depends on the individual’s needs, goals, frequency of sessions and progress. The plan can be reviewed over time with the person and, where appropriate, their family.

Can parents or family members be involved?

Where appropriate, family members can help share observations, understand strategies and support practice in everyday routines. The level of involvement is discussed based on the person’s age, preferences and goals.

Do I need a referral to get started?

Referral requirements can vary by provider and circumstance. Contact Priyanka to ask about arranging an initial discussion and whether any referral or previous reports would be useful.
